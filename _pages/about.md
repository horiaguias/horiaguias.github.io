<h2 id="working-papers" style="font-size: 1.3rem; font-weight: 600; margin: 0.5rem 0; border-bottom: none;">Job Market Paper</h2>

<h3 style="font-size: 1.1rem; font-weight: 600; margin-bottom: 0.4rem;">Perceived Responsibility for Negative Externalities</h3>
<p class="paper-links">
  <a href="/jmp" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Download Paper</a> | <a href="https://github.com/horiaguias/Responsibility-Functions-Experimental-Code">Experimental Code</a>
</p>

<details>
  <summary>Abstract</summary>
  <p>
    Why do some individuals pay to offset their own emissions but not the same emissions caused by someone else? I study how perceived responsibility for negative externalities -- shaped by previously caused harm versus anticipated consequences -- affects decision-making. In a stylized theoretical framework, a decision maker can repair self-caused harm and exogenous background harm of unknown origin. Beyond outcome-based preferences, she cares about maintaining a moral self-image that is activated by backward-looking responsibility. Under certain conditions, a desire to restore that moral self-image overrides efficiency or equity concerns, yet the decision maker does not feel responsible for background harm. Based on this insight, I propose a sharp revealed preference test that isolates responsibility-based preferences from outcome-based preferences. It informs the design of a lab experiment in which I vary the causal history of harm and the price to repair self-caused and background harm. Structural estimation of preferences reveals considerable heterogeneity in the influence of perceived responsibility on behaviour at the individual and aggregate level. At the aggregate level, subjects exhibit a responsibility premium of 29%--44% when paying to repair self-caused harm. These results have important implications for the limits of individual responsibility in consumer markets, the demand for voluntary carbon offsets, and the efficiency of CSR activities.
  </p>
</details>

<h2 id="working-papers" style="font-size: 1.3rem; font-weight: 600; margin: 0.5rem 0; border-bottom: none;">Other Working Papers</h2>


<h3 style="font-size: 1.1rem; font-weight: 600; margin-bottom: 0.4rem;">Price-Discrimination with Responsibility-Sensitive Consumers</h3>
<p class="paper-links">
  <a href="/iopaper" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Download Paper</a>
</p>

<details>
  <summary>Abstract</summary>
  <p>
    In this paper, I characterize market equilibria if the seller has access to an offsetting technology to mitigate the pollution that is generated by the consumption and production of the core product it sells. In contrast to an intrinsic environmental quality, offsets do not tie the environmental quality to the core product sold to a consumer, but they also allow consumers to pay to reduce the pollution emitted by the core product sold to other consumers. I show that whether and how a monopolistic seller employs these offsets in equilibrium depends on the nature of the environmental preferences of conscious consumers. If consumers have responsibility-based preferences and care exclusively about the net pollution of their own consumption good, the seller maintains high demand and profits by offering no offsets to selfish consumers and full offsets for the pollution of the core product sold to environmentally concerned consumers. Because the pollution emitted by the core product sold to selfish consumers is not mitigated, this market outcome is inefficient, and a benevolent social planner wants to correct this inefficiency by setting a carbon tax, which works as a mandatory offset for all core products. In contrast, if consumers have outcome-based preferences and care about the total pollution emitted in the market, a seller benefits by expanding the product line and offering the most environmentally concerned consumers the possibility to pay to offset the pollution emitted by the goods sold to selfish consumers. Under certain conditions, the aggregate amount of pollution can be offset. Work in progress extends the results to a duopolistic setting.
  </p>
</details>
