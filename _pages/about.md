<h3 style="font-size: 1.1rem; font-weight: 600; margin-bottom: 0.4rem;">Perceived Responsibility for Negative Externalities</h3>
<p class="paper-links">
  <a href="/jmp.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Download Paper</a> | <a href="https://github.com/horiaguias/Responsibility-Functions-Experimental-Code">Experimental Code</a>
</p>

<details>
  <summary>Abstract</summary>
  <p>
    Why do some individuals pay to offset their own emissions but not the same emissions caused by someone else? I study how perceived responsibility for negative externalities -- shaped by previously caused harm versus anticipated consequences -- affects decision-making. In a stylized theoretical framework, a decision maker can repair self-caused harm and exogenous background harm of unknown origin. Beyond outcome-based preferences, she cares about maintaining a moral self-image that is activated by backward-looking responsibility. Under certain conditions, a desire to restore that moral self-image overrides efficiency or equity concerns, yet the decision maker does not feel responsible for background harm. Based on this insight, I propose a sharp revealed preference test that isolates responsibility-based preferences from outcome-based preferences. It informs the design of a lab experiment in which I vary the causal history of harm and the price to repair self-caused and background harm. Structural estimation of preferences reveals considerable heterogeneity in the influence of perceived responsibility on behaviour at the individual and aggregate level. At the aggregate level, subjects exhibit a responsibility premium of 29%--44% when paying to repair self-caused harm. These results have important implications for the limits of individual responsibility in consumer markets, the demand for voluntary carbon offsets, and the efficiency of CSR activities.
  </p>
</details>
